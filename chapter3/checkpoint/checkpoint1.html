Checkpoint 1.1

Answers from Chapter 1 of the textbook.

1. Describe the difference between the Internet and the Web.

The Internet is the actual network - it's millions of computers all connected together around the world. It's been around since the late 1960s when the U.S. government started linking research computers. The Web came later in the early 1990s and it's basically just one way to use the Internet. It's the graphical side of things - websites, pages, links, images. You access the Web through a browser, but the Internet itself also carries other stuff like email (SMTP), file transfers (FTP), and more. So the Web runs on top of the Internet, but they're not the same thing.

2. Explain three events that contributed to the commercialization and exponential growth of the Internet.

First, the ban on commercial activity on the Internet was lifted in the early 1990s, which meant businesses could finally start using it. Before that it was mostly just for universities and government research. Second, Tim Berners-Lee created the World Wide Web at CERN, which gave people a way to browse information with a graphical interface instead of just command lines. Third, the Mosaic web browser was developed at NCSA - it was the first browser that could show images alongside text, which made the Web way more accessible to regular people. On top of all that, personal computers were getting cheaper and ISPs started offering affordable dial-up connections, so more people could actually get online.

3. Is the concept of universal design important to web developers? Explain your answer.

Yes, universal design is really important for web developers. The idea behind it is designing products and environments so they work for as many people as possible without needing special modifications. For the Web, that means making sites that are usable by people with different abilities - someone using a screen reader, someone who can't use a mouse, someone with low vision, etc. There are also legal reasons: the ADA and Section 508 require certain organizations to make their digital content accessible. But even beyond the legal side, it just makes good sense. If your site is hard to use for some people, you're losing visitors. The W3C has the Web Accessibility Initiative (WAI) and the WCAG guidelines that lay out how to do this properly.
